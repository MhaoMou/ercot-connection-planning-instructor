game is, how your plan is scored, how to explore the data, how to write and

\section{The game in one page}\label{sec:game}

Your team is a data-center operator. You choose where to connect computing
load to a public-data-informed planning model of the Texas grid, how large (in MW) connection is, and
whether to build your own generation and battery storage next to it. The
instructor, acting as the grid operator, then runs one DCOPF dispatch with every team's plan in it, and scores each team. The grid is the public ACTIVSg2000 test
system~\cite{activsg2000}: 2,000 buses, 3,206 branches, and 544 generators,
stored in the MATPOWER case format~\cite{matpower}.

\subsection{What you decide}
Your plan is a list of \emph{sites}. For each site you choose four numbers:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\toprule
Decision & Unit & Meaning\\
\midrule
Bus & -- & Where the site connects to the network (a \code{bus_id}).\\
Connection capacity $C$ & MW & Your requested computing load cap at this site. \\
Generator capacity $G$ & MW & Optional private generator at the site, at most $C$.\\
Storage power $P$ / energy $B$ & MW / MWh & Optional battery: either both zero, or $B = 4P$.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

You only choose sites and capacities. The grid operator decides how your
generator and battery run in each six-hour operating period. Your generator and battery serve only
your own load: they never export to the grid or to another team.

\subsection{Schedule}
The game runs for three weeks with each week focusing on the data-center planning for the year of 2027, 2028, and 2029, respectively. Each week has two submissions, and each
submission covers the plan for one half-year with six-hour operating intervals. Each
operating interval represents six consecutive real hours. The first week is for
practice and the results will not be counted for grades. The second and third
weeks are for real game playing and the results will be counted for grades.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X r r r@{}}
\toprule
Week & Submission 1 & Submission 2 & Counts toward final grade?\\
\midrule
1 & 2027 Jan--Jun & 2027 Jul--Dec & \textcolor{red}{\(\times\)}\\
2 & 2028 Jan--Jun & 2028 Jul--Dec & \textcolor{green!60!black}{\(\checkmark\)}\\
3 & 2029 Jan--Jun & 2029 Jul--Dec & \textcolor{green!60!black}{\(\checkmark\)}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The released six-hour half-year horizons are 728 operating periods for
January--June 2028, 724 periods for January--June 2027 and 2029, and 736
periods for every July--December. These correspond to 4,368, 4,344, and
4,416 real hours, respectively. All flow-based revenues and costs are charged
for six hours per operating period; capacities and rates remain in MW, MWh,
and \$/MWh.
The canonical release uses the scenario packages under
\code{data/processed/activsg2000\_scenarios\_v2\_6h/} and the matching
\code{dispatch\_scoring\_rules\_*\_6h\_v1.json} files. \textbf{The retired hourly submission format is not current: do not
submit one CSV row per operating period.} Your CSV remains one row per
connection site; the evaluator expands that site-level plan across the
released operating periods.

\begin{keybox}[The six-hour convention]
The source profiles are hourly. For each game period $t$, the released package
averages six consecutive source hours:
\[
  \bar{x}_t = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{h=6t}^{6t+5} x_h.
\]
The evaluator then charges six hours of service, energy, and operating cost for
that period. A day therefore contains four game periods. With
\code{daily\_exact\_cycle}, storage starts at $B/2$ and must end every day at
exactly $B/2$; the grid operator chooses its signed charging/discharging power.
\end{keybox}
\textbf{Your final score is the sum of your four submission scores from
Weeks 2 and 3}.

Each of these horizons is an integer number of 24-hour dispatch blocks.

Within a week, the two submissions are linked:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Sites, generators, and storages built in Submission 1 \textbf{stay built} in
    Submission 2. The battery's installed capacity remains available, but its
    operating state is reset to half full at the start of each daily dispatch
    block.
  \item You cannot move or resize a site you built in Submission 1, but you
    can add new sites in Submission 2.
  \item Capital cost is charged once, in the submission where an asset is
    first built. Submission 2 does not pay again for what you built in
    Submission 1.
  \item Everything \textbf{resets} at the start of the next week.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}
A site built in Submission 1 is paid for once but earns IT value in both
half-years of the week. A site added in Submission 2 costs the same and earns
for only one half-year.
\end{keybox}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/pipeline.tex}
  \caption{What you receive and what you submit. The released package gives
  you the network plus scenario context and validation inputs. You create the
  plan (CSV submission), and can preview it yourself, but only the official evaluator

  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\section{How your score is computed}\label{sec:score}

For each submission, your score in US dollars is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \text{score} = \text{IT value}
  &- \text{energy cost} - \text{generator operating cost}\\
  &- \text{generator capital} - \text{storage capital} - \text{site cost}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
\toprule
Term & Value & How it is charged\\
\midrule
IT value & \$6,500 per MWh & Paid for every MWh of your load \textbf{that is actually served}.\\
Energy cost & \textbf{L}ocational \textbf{M}arginal \textbf{P}rice (LMP) at your site & Grid purchases times the LMP and six-hour interval length. \textbf{Submission 2 only; Submission 1 has no energy cost.}\\
Generator operating cost & \$80 / \$150 / \$250 per MWh & Piecewise: first 25\% of capacity at \$80, next 50\% at \$150, last 25\% at \$250.\\
Generator capital & \$1.5M per MW & On generator capacity, in the submission where it is first built.\\
Storage capital & \$400k per MW + \$250k per MWh & In the submission where it is first built. A 4-hour battery costs \$1.4M per MW of power.\\
Site cost & \$25M per MW & On connection capacity, in the submission where the site is first built.\\
\bottomrule
\label{tab:hyperparameters}
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\textbf{Storage has no operating cost.} A file that fails validation scores 0.

\subsection{A worked example}\label{subsec:worked_example}
Suppose in Week 2 you build one 50\MW{} site in Submission 1 and nothing else,
and all of its load is served in both submissions. Baseline LMPs in July--December
are about \$18/MWh.

\textbf{Submission 1} (2028 January--June, 728 six-hour periods, no energy cost):
\begin{align*}
  \text{IT value}  &= 50 \times 728 \times 6 \times 6{,}500 = \$1{,}419.6\text{M}\\
  \text{site cost} &= 50 \times 25\text{M} = \$1{,}250.0\text{M}\\
  \text{score}     &= \$169.6\text{M}
\end{align*}

\textbf{Submission 2} (2028 July--December, 736 six-hour periods, site already paid for):
\begin{align*}
  \text{IT value}    &= 50 \times 736 \times 6 \times 6{,}500 = \$1{,}435.2\text{M}\\
  \text{energy cost} &\approx 50 \times 736 \times 6 \times 18 = \$4.0\text{M}\\
  \text{score}       &\approx \$1{,}431\text{M}
\end{align*}

The week adds about \$1,601M to your final score. Over the week, each served MW
earns about \$57M against a one-time site cost of \$25M, so a site built in
Submission 1 breaks even once about \textbf{44\% of its load is served}. A site
added in Submission 2 earns only about \$28.6M per MW for the same \$25M, so it
needs about \textbf{87\% served} to break even.

\begin{keybox}
Energy is cheap compared with the value of serving load (the IT value, 6,500\$/MW). What decides your score is \textbf{whether the grid can actually deliver power to your site.} Load the
network cannot serve still pays the full site cost.
\end{keybox}

\begin{exercisebox}
A team builds a 30\MW{} site in Submission 1 of Week 3 (2029 January--June,
724 six-hour periods) and it is fully served in both submissions (Submission 2
is 2029 July--December, 736 periods, LMP about \$18/MWh). Estimate the two
scores. \emph{(Answer: Submission 1 is $30 \times 724 \times 6 \times
6{,}500 - 750\text{M} \approx \$97\text{M}$; Submission 2 is
$30 \times 736 \times 6 \times (6{,}500 - 18) \approx \$859\text{M}$.)}
\end{exercisebox}

\subsection{You are not the only player}\label{sec:projection}
The worked example in Section \ref{subsec:worked_example} quietly assumed that all of your load is served. Whether it
is depends on more than your own plan. Every team reads the same rules and does
the same arithmetic, so every team is tempted by the same plan: build big,
build early, and pick buses (sites) where the network looks strong. Each of those plans
can be fine on its own, and your preview, which runs without the other teams,
will say so. But \textbf{the spare capacity of the grid is shared}. If too many teams
crowd the same part of the network, the combined plan has no feasible dispatch,
even though no single plan caused the problem.

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``The hard thing in life is getting you to come out of your own selves
a bit, realizing it's `not all about you.'\,''
\end{quote}

When the combined plan of all teams is infeasible, the evaluator has to decide who gets
served. It does not use first-come-first-served. It applies a \textbf{fair
projection}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Caps.} Each site's request is capped at 100\MW{} and each team's
    at 500\MW{}. With several teams, the largest team's request is also capped
    at the smallest positive team request.
  \item \textbf{Service fractions.} Each team $i$ gets a single fraction
    $\alpha_i \in [0,1]$ that scales its connection capacities of all sites of the half-year.
  \item \textbf{Objective.} The evaluator first maximizes total served load,
    then, among near-optimal solutions, maximizes $\sum_i \log(\alpha_i d_i)$
    (proportional fairness).
\end{enumerate}

This changes the question you should ask. Your served load, and so
whether you clear the break-even points above, depends on what everyone else
builds. A bigger request does not buy you priority: the caps cut
it down to the smallest team's size, while capital is still charged on the full
capacity you submitted. So the question is not only ``how much load can my
site take?'' but also ``how much room will be left for me once the other teams
have built, and what do they expect me to do?''

\Cref{sec:twoteam} works through a stylized two-team example at two
neighboring buses of this grid.

\begin{exercisebox}[Think about it]
Suppose you expect most teams to crowd the best-connected buses. What do you
gain and what do you risk by choosing a less popular location instead? What
would the Week 1 results tell you about how other teams actually behave?
\end{exercisebox}

\section{How the grid operator runs your plan}\label{sec:model}

The evaluator solves a DCOPF on the ACTIVSg2000 network for every six-hour
operating period of the half-year, with all teams' sites added. The existing
network, generators, and base loads come from the scenario block. \Cref{fig:site}
shows what one row of your CSV adds at a bus $k$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/student_vs_evaluator.tex}
  \caption{One site in the dispatch. The site draws $\alpha C$ of IT load; your
  generator and battery can cover part of it, and the rest is bought from the
  grid at the bus LMP.}
  \label{fig:site}
\end{figure}

For a site at bus $k$ in aggregate period $t$, the evaluator adds:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a fixed load $\alpha C$ (normally $\alpha \in [0,1]$, cf. Section~\ref{sec:projection});
  \item a private generator $0 \le g_t \le G$ with the piecewise cost in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters};
  \item a battery with signed power $p_t$ (positive = discharge), limited by
    $-P \le p_t \le P$.
\end{itemize}
Your grid purchase rate in period $t$ is $\alpha C - g_t - p_t$. The generator and
battery can only cover your own team's load at that bus; they cannot push power
back into the grid.

\subsection{Storage}
Each battery follows the model below, with six-hour steps and 100\% efficiency.
The signed variable $p_{d,h}$ is a power rate in MW: positive means discharge
and negative means charge. Let $\Delta t=6$ hours.
\begin{gather*}
  e_{d,h} = e_{d,h-1} - \Delta t\,p_{d,h}, \qquad 0 \le e_{d,h} \le B, \qquad -P \le p_{d,h} \le P, \qquad \Delta t\,p_{d,h} \le e_{d,h-1},\\
  e_{d,0} = 0.5B, \qquad e_{d,4} = 0.5B \qquad \text{for each day }d.
\end{gather*}
The battery starts every daily dispatch block half full and must end that block
at exactly half full after four six-hour periods (\cref{fig:soc}). There is no state-of-charge carryover
from one day to the next. Discharge at a bus cannot exceed your own load there.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/storage_soc.tex}
  \caption{An illustrative one-day battery schedule with $B = 4P$: it starts
  at $0.5B$, stays between $0$ and $B$, moves by at most $6P$ MWh per
  six-hour interval, and returns to exactly $0.5B$ after four intervals. The evaluator
  chooses this schedule independently for each day.}
  \label{fig:soc}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Prices}
The LMP at your bus is the dual of that bus's power balance in the joint
dispatch, clipped at zero. In the canonical six-hour release, Submission 2
settles against LMPs computed from the actual joint dispatch containing all
accepted teams. Optional baseline LMP artifacts may be generated for
diagnostics, but they are not required for scoring. With teams' load added,
lines can congest and the LMP at your bus can rise.

\begin{exercisebox}
A battery has $P = 5\MW$ and $B = 20\MWh$. It starts at the required initial
state of charge for a daily block. Can it discharge at 5\MW{} for two
six-hour operating periods in a row? What is the state of charge after each
period? \emph{(Answer: it starts at 10\MWh, is at 0\MWh{} after two periods;
it cannot discharge during a third period until it has charged. It must also
return to exactly 10\MWh{} by the end of that 24-hour block.)}
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Using AI for this assignment}\label{sec:ai}

You are encouraged to use AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, GitHub
Copilot, Claude Code, or Codex. You are still responsible for every number you
submit and must be able to explain your plan. This section is for anyone who
has not used these tools much.

\subsection{Which kind of tool}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Chat assistants} (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini in a browser) are best
    for explaining concepts, writing short scripts, and decoding error
    messages. They only see what you paste in. Most of them can be used for free. However, without subscriptions, we may be limited to only the use of basic models.
  \item \textbf{Coding assistants} (Claude Code, Codex, Copilot in your editor)
    can read the GitHub repository and run commands for you. They are faster once you
    are comfortable, but check what they change. Most of them require subscriptions.
\end{itemize}
If you are new to this, start with a chat assistant and copy code into your
own files. Each tool has an official getting-started guide:

\subsection{How to write a good prompt}
The AI knows DCOPF but knows nothing about this assignment. Give it four
things: the \textbf{goal}, the \textbf{context} (paste file headers, a few
rows, error messages), the \textbf{constraints}, and the \textbf{form} of answer
you want.

\begin{promptbox}[Weak prompt]
Write code to analyze the data.
\end{promptbox}

\begin{promptbox}[Better prompt]
I am working in Python with pandas. I have a file \code{branch_limits.csv} with
columns \msg{branch_id, from_bus_id, to_bus_id, reactance_pu, thermal_limit_mva, status}.
Here are its first three rows: [paste]. Write code that, for each bus, adds
up \code{thermal_limit_mva} over in-service branches (\msg{status == 1})
connected to that bus, and prints the 20 buses with the largest total.
Explain each line briefly.
\end{promptbox}

\subsection{Work in a loop}
Treat the AI as a pair programmer. Ask for something small, run it, and paste
back exactly what happened, including the full error message. Repeat until it
works, then ask it to explain the final version so you understand it.

\begin{promptbox}
I ran your script and got this error:
\msg{KeyError: 'thermal_limit'}. The column names are exactly:
\code{branch_id,from_bus_id,to_bus_id,reactance_pu,thermal_limit_mva,status}.
Please fix the script.
\end{promptbox}

\begin{aibox}
The column is called \code{thermal_limit_mva}, not \code{thermal_limit}.
Change line 6 to \code{branches["thermal_limit_mva"]} and run it again.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{Check what the AI tells you}
AI tools are often confidently wrong about assignment-specific details. They
might invent a CSV column, assume you schedule the battery yourself, or use
made-up prices. Before you rely on an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Run it.} Code that has not run is a guess.
  \item \textbf{Check the numbers} against the handout and the rules JSON.
  \item \textbf{Try a case you can do by hand}, such as the worked example in
    \cref{sec:score}.
  \item \textbf{Ask for its assumptions}: ``What did you assume that is not in

\subsection{Hosting capacity: how much room a bus has}\label{sec:hc}
The ``room'' has a name. The \textbf{hosting capacity} of a bus
is the largest additional load that can be connected there without making the
grid fail, that is, without making the DCOPF infeasible, while everything else
in the network stays the same~\cite{bollen2011integration,wan2026hosting}.
Because your site draws the same load every six-hour operating period, what matters is the
\emph{smallest} hosting capacity over the half-year, which usually occurs in the
aggregate periods with the highest system load.

Here are hosting capacities for a few buses in the 2028 July--December block,
computed with the code in \cref{app:hc} for period 0 and for period 306, the
six-hour period with the highest system load:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l r r@{}}
\toprule
Bus & Period 0 & Peak period\\
\midrule
1001 & 375\MW & 334\MW\\
1002 & 127\MW & 123\MW\\
7419 & 598\MW & 278\MW\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Hosting capacity tells you where you can build and roughly how much}.
Use it as a screening calculation, not as a guarantee: the official result
also includes other teams and the dispatch of team-built assets.

\begin{warningbox}
Hosting capacity is computed as if your site were the only new load. Other
teams' sites use up the same headroom. At the peak period, bus 1002 can host
123\MW{} on its own, but only 82\MW{} if another team connects 60\MW{} at the
neighboring bus 1010. The hosting capacities of different buses do not add up,
and the busier a part of the network becomes, the less room each site there
has (\cref{sec:projection}).
\end{warningbox}

\Cref{app:hc} gives the optimization behind hosting capacity, how to compute
it with the GitHub repository's own DCOPF solver, and how private generation and
storage change it.

\section{Develop a strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

There is no single right answer, but the scoring rules point to a few
questions worth answering with data.

\textbf{When to build?} Build in Submission 1. A site built then earns IT
value for the whole year and is paid for once; the same site added in
Submission 2 earns for half the time. Use Submission 2 for additions you are
confident the network can serve.

\textbf{How big?} A site built in Submission 1 earns about \$32M per MW over
the week after site cost if fully served, and breaks even at about 44\%
served. Grow capacity in steps and watch where served load stops keeping
up.

\textbf{Where?} Since baseline prices are flat, location matters through
network headroom. Shortlist buses with a large hosting capacity
(\cref{sec:hc}), keep your request comfortably below it to leave room for other
teams, and preview the same capacity at several of them.

\textbf{Private generation?} At \$80/MWh or more, your generator costs more
than baseline grid energy (about \$18/MWh), so in an uncongested operating period it sits
idle and its \$1.5M/MW capital is lost. It pays off when it lets more of your
load be served at a congested location, or when congestion pushes your LMP
above its cost.

\textbf{Storage?} With nearly flat prices, there is little to gain from buying
low and selling high. Like a generator, a battery is mainly useful at a
location where the network is tight in some operating periods. Remember that the preview
does not dispatch storage, so it will not show this benefit.

\textbf{Other teams.} Your preview runs without them, but the official
dispatch includes everyone. If the combined plan is infeasible, the caps in
\cref{sec:projection} cut sites above 100\MW{} and teams larger than the
smallest team first, and you still pay for everything you submitted.

\textbf{Use Week 1.} Week 1 does not count. Use it to see how your plan is
treated in the joint evaluation with everyone else.

\subsection{A two-team example}\label{sec:twoteam}
Here is the game in miniature. Team A builds
at bus 1002 and team B at the neighboring bus 1010, and each chooses between a
small (50\MW) and a large (100\MW) site. On its own, either bus could take
100\MW: at the peak period their hosting capacities are 123 and 136\MW{}
(\cref{sec:hc}). Together they cannot. To keep the arithmetic simple, assume
every six-hour operating period of the week looks like the 2028 July--December peak
period, both
teams build in Submission 1, and service is decided by the fair projection
(\cref{sec:projection}). Each served MW is then worth about \$57.0M over the
week, and each built MW costs \$25M.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c@{}}
\toprule
Payoffs per week, A / B (\$M) & B small (50\MW) & B large (100\MW)\\
\midrule
A small (50\MW) & 1,601 / 1,601 & 1,601 / 351\\
A large (100\MW) & 351 / 1,601 & 3,202 / $-39$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Both small.} The grid can serve both sites in full.
  \item \textbf{One large, one small.} The combined plan is infeasible. The
    caps cut the large request down to the other team's 50\MW, so both teams
    get 50\MW{} served, but the large team still pays for 100\MW.
  \item \textbf{Both large.} The plan is infeasible, and the caps change
    nothing because the requests are equal. The projection maximizes total
    served load. Each MW served at bus 1002 uses less of the shared
    bottleneck than a MW at bus 1010, so A is served in full and B gets only
    43.2\MW.
\end{itemize}

Team B should go small whatever A does: 1,601 beats both 351 and $-39$.
Knowing that, A's best reply is also small. So (small, small) is the stable
outcome, the Nash equilibrium of this game (\cref{app:game}). A would do far
better if both went large, but B will never cooperate with that. Three lessons
carry over to the real game: over-requesting next to another team is punished
by the caps; when the grid is short, your position in the network decides who
gets served; and your best plan depends on what you expect the others to do.

This example simplifies the real evaluator, which uses every six-hour
operating period of the half-year and every team's sites at once.

\section{After each submission: reading your results}\label{sec:results}

After every submission the instructor releases:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item the \textbf{score breakdown}: IT value, energy cost, generator
    operating cost, and the capital costs charged in that submission;
  \item the \textbf{service fraction} $\alpha$ of every team;
  \item whether the \textbf{fair projection} was triggered;
  \item an \textbf{interactive grid map} (SVG) drawn on a schematic layout
    of the network (not a geographic map). Each bus is colored by the
    total connection capacity that all teams placed there together, and each
    line by how heavily it was loaded. Hover over a bus or line to see the
    numbers. Only totals per bus are shown, so the map does not say who built
    what.
\end{itemize}

Read them in this order:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Was the projection triggered?} If not, the combined plan fit
    the grid and every team has $\alpha = 1$. If it was, compare your
    $\alpha$ with the other teams'.
  \item \textbf{Is your $\alpha$ above break-even?} A site built in
    Submission 1 needs about 88\% served to pay for itself within that
    submission, and about 44\% over the whole week. A site added in
    Submission 2 needs about 87\% (\cref{sec:score}).
  \item \textbf{Which term drives your score?} Usually the capital costs in
    Submission 1 and the IT value in Submission 2.
  \item \textbf{How does it compare with your preview?} If much less of your
    load was served than in the preview, the shortfall came from other teams'
    sites, which your preview did not include.
  \item \textbf{Where is the grid crowded?} On the map, look for busy buses and
    heavily loaded lines near your sites, and for regions that still look
    quiet.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Planning the next submission.} After Submission 1, your sites are
fixed and you can only add new ones. To see how much room is left, add the
published per-bus totals to the hosting-capacity calculation as extra load:
pass them in \code{additional_demand_mw_by_bus} together with your candidate
(\cref{app:hc}). After a week, everything resets, but remember that the other
teams have learned from the results too.

\section{Frequently asked questions}\label{sec:faq}

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=6pt]
\item[Why is my score negative?]
  Stay calm, especially after Submission 1. Submission 1 is the building stage:
  it pays the full capital cost of every site, generator, and battery you
  build, while earning IT value for only half a year. Submission 2 pays no
  capital for what you already built. For example, a 50\MW{} site that is
  80\% served scores about $-\$114$M in Submission 1, but about $+\$1{,}145$M
  in Submission 2, so the week adds about \$1,031M. Judge a week by the sum of
  both submissions. A negative score in Submission 2, or a large negative
  total for the week, is a real warning sign: check your $\alpha$ and the map.
\item[Is the preview my score?]
  No. It samples every 6th six-hour period (every 36 hours), leaves batteries idle, ignores other teams,
  and charges capital for every site in the file (\cref{sec:preview}). Use it to
  compare your own candidates.
\item[Does Week 1 count?]
  No. Weeks 2 and 3 count, and both submissions in each of those weeks count.
\item[Do I pay for energy in Submission 1?]
  No. Energy is paid at the LMP in Submission 2 only.
\item[Can I schedule my battery or sell power from my generator?]
  No. The evaluator dispatches both, and they can only serve your own load.
\item[What if I leave my Submission 1 rows out of Submission 2?]
  Those sites stay built and keep drawing load. Keep the rows unchanged anyway,
  so that your file describes your whole plan.
\item[Can I resize or move a site in Submission 2?]
  No. You can only add new sites, generators, and batteries.
\item[What happens if my file is invalid?]
  It scores 0 for that submission. Other teams are evaluated without it. Run the
  checker on the exact file you upload.
\item[Can other teams see my plan?]
  Not in detail. They see the score breakdown, every team's $\alpha$, and the
  total connection capacity at each bus, not who built what.
\item[My generator never seems to run. Is something wrong?]
  Probably not. At \$80/MWh or more it costs more than grid energy at about
  \$18/MWh, so the operator only uses it when the grid cannot deliver enough
  to your bus, or when congestion pushes the price at your bus above its cost.
\end{description}

\section{Hosting capacity in detail}\label{app:hc}

The hosting-capacity idea comes from distribution planning, where it measures
how much distributed generation a feeder can absorb before its performance
becomes unacceptable~\cite{bollen2011integration}. The same idea applies to
load. Recent work uses it to ask how large a data center a transmission bus can
accept~\cite{wan2026hosting}, and treats it as something that changes over
time rather than a single number~\cite{deoliveira2019dynamic}. In this appendix, we 
state a version that matches this game.

\subsection{Formulation}
Use the notation of the per-period DCOPF: buses $i$, generators $g$ with limits
$[p_g^{\min}, p_g^{\max}]$, branches $\ell$ from $o(\ell)$ to $r(\ell)$ with
susceptance $b_\ell$ and thermal limit $F_\ell$, base load $d_{i,t}$, and fixed
DC-line injections $s_{i,t}$. The hosting capacity of bus $k$ in operating period $t$ is
the optimal value of the linear program
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{HC}_{k,t} = \max_{L,\,p,\,\theta,\,f} \quad & L\\
  \text{s.t.}\quad
  & \sum_{g \text{ at } i} p_g + s_{i,t} - d_{i,t} - L\,\mathbbm{1}\{i=k\}
    = \sum_{\ell:\,o(\ell)=i} f_\ell - \sum_{\ell:\,r(\ell)=i} f_\ell
    && \forall i,\\
  & f_\ell = b_\ell\,(\theta_{o(\ell)} - \theta_{r(\ell)}),\quad
    -F_\ell \le f_\ell \le F_\ell && \forall \ell,\\
  & p_g^{\min} \le p_g \le p_g^{\max} && \forall g,\\
  & \theta_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0,\qquad L \ge 0 .
\end{align*}
These are exactly the DCOPF constraints with one extra load $L$ at bus $k$. The
generation-cost objective is replaced by ``make $L$ as large as possible''.
At the optimum some branch is at its thermal limit, or the generators are at
their limits. That binding constraint is the bottleneck for bus $k$.

\paragraph{Over the half-year.} Your site's load is the same in every operating
period, so
the number that matters for a plan is
\[
  \mathrm{HC}_k = \min_t \mathrm{HC}_{k,t}.
\]
This equals one large LP with a single $L$ shared by every operating period. In practice
you compute $\mathrm{HC}_{k,t}$ for the periods with the highest system load. The
minimum over a sample of periods is an \emph{upper} bound on $\mathrm{HC}_k$, so
check more periods before relying on a number close to the limit.

\paragraph{Several sites at once.} For new loads $L_1, \dots, L_n$ at several
buses, the set of combinations that keep every operating period feasible is a convex
polytope, the \emph{hosting-capacity region}. Each single-bus hosting capacity
is where this region meets one axis. When two buses share a bottleneck, the
region is much smaller than the box formed by their individual hosting
capacities. At a peak period, two neighboring buses may each look adequate
on their own, but the second request can reduce the room available at the
first. The fair projection in
\cref{sec:projection} picks a point in the joint hosting-capacity region of all
teams' sites, which is why your service depends on where everyone else builds.

\paragraph{Private generation and storage.} A private generator of $G$\MW{} at
bus $k$ can cover up to $G$ of the site's load locally, so in any operating period the site
can host up to $\mathrm{HC}_{k,t} + G$. You pay for this through capital and
an operating cost of at least \$80/MWh whenever the generator runs. A battery
can do the same for a few operating periods at a time: it helps when the hosting capacity
falls below your load only in a short window, as long as it has enough power
($P \ge L - \mathrm{HC}_{k,t}$) and enough stored energy to cover the gap.

\paragraph{Further reading: flexible connection.} In this game your load is
fixed: the site draws its full capacity in every operating period, so a single tight period
sets $\mathrm{HC}_k$. Gu, Chen, and Qin~\cite{gu2026flexible} ask what happens
if a new load instead agrees to be \textbf{curtailed} in a small number of time slots
per year, an arrangement called \emph{flexible connection}. The hosting
capacity is then set by the slots that remain. For a constant load at one bus,
allowing the load to be curtailed in at most $K$ operating periods gives
\[
  \mathrm{HC}_k^{(K)} = \mathrm{HC}_{k,[K+1]},
\]
the $(K+1)$-th smallest of the operating-period hosting capacities $\mathrm{HC}_{k,t}$:
you are no longer limited by the $K$ tightest periods. Their distribution-feeder
case study finds that curtailing in only 1\% of the 15-minute slots of a year
(about 88 hours) raises the hosting capacity by about 78\%, that the gain grows
more slowly as $K$ increases, and that most required curtailments are shallow.
Letting the load delay its consumption instead of dropping it unlocks at most as
much. A follow-up paper extends the approach to grids where only some buses are
measured~\cite{gu2026partial}.

You cannot curtail in this game, but your own generator or battery can play the
same role: whatever the grid cannot deliver in the $K$ tightest periods must come
from on-site resources, that is, $C - \mathrm{HC}_{k,t}$\MW{} in each of those
periods. To see whether that is worth it at a bus you like, compute
$\mathrm{HC}_{k,t}$ for the peak periods, sort them, and compare the smallest
value with, say, the 25th smallest. The difference is the extra capacity that
covering the 24 tightest periods would unlock, and the gap in each of those periods
tells you the generator or battery power you would need.

\paragraph{What this version leaves out.} The game's evaluator uses a plain
DCOPF, so this formulation has no N-1 contingency or reserve constraints, which
real interconnection studies include~\cite{wan2026hosting}. It also ignores
losses and voltage limits, which the DC approximation does not model.

\subsection{Computing it}
You do not need to write the LP yourself. Since the set of feasible $L$ is an
interval $[0, \mathrm{HC}_{k,t}]$, a bisection that asks ``is the DCOPF still
feasible with $L$ extra MW at bus $k$?'' finds the hosting capacity. The
GitHub repository's solver answers that question directly; each check takes about
0.2 seconds on a laptop. Run from the GitHub repository root:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=tutorialpython]
from tools.activsg2000.dcopf import DCOPFInfeasible, DCOPFUnresolved, solve_prepared_period

block = "data/processed/activsg2000_scenarios_v2_6h/2028/jul_dec"


def feasible(bus_id, extra_mw, period):
    """True if the grid can serve extra_mw at bus_id in this period."""
    try:
        solve_prepared_period(block, period_index=period,
                              additional_demand_mw_by_bus={bus_id: extra_mw})
        return True
    except (DCOPFInfeasible, DCOPFUnresolved):
        return False  # near the limit HiGHS may not resolve; count it as "no"


def hosting_capacity(bus_id, period, upper_mw=1000.0, tol_mw=1.0):
    """Largest extra load at bus_id that keeps this period feasible."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, upper_mw
    if feasible(bus_id, hi, period):
        return hi                      # at least upper_mw
    while hi - lo > tol_mw:            # feasible loads form an interval [0, HC]
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if feasible(bus_id, mid, period):
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return lo


peak_period = 306  # highest system load in 2028 July-December (see below)
for bus in ["1001", "1002"]:
    print(bus, [round(hosting_capacity(bus, period)) for period in (0, peak_period)])
\end{lstlisting}
Output:
\begin{lstlisting}
1001 [375, 334]
1002 [127, 123]
\end{lstlisting}

To find the operating periods with the highest system load, add up the
periodic bus loads:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=tutorialpython]
from ercot_game.scenario_profiles import hourly_state

totals = []
for period in range(736):  # 736 six-hour periods in July-December
    # hourly_state returns the six-hour mean state for this game period
    demand, _ = hourly_state(block, year=2028, calendar_block="jul_dec", period_index=period)
    totals.append(sum(demand.values()))
peak_periods = sorted(range(len(totals)), key=lambda p: -totals[p])[:24]
peak_period = peak_periods[0]
print(peak_periods[:5])
\end{lstlisting}

To check how a neighbor affects you, pass both loads at once inside
\code{feasible}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=tutorialpython]

\end{lstlisting}

(\cref{sec:preview}).

\section{The evaluator in full}\label{app:model}

This appendix writes out the problem the evaluator solves for one
half-year.

\paragraph{Sets and data.} Buses $n$, branches $\ell$ from $o(\ell)$ to
$r(\ell)$ with susceptance $b_\ell$ and thermal limit $F_\ell$, existing
generators $g$ with limits $[p_g^{\min}, p_g^{\max}]$ and marginal cost $c_g$,
base load $d_{n,t}$, fixed DC-line injections $s_{n,t}$, and six-hour
operating periods
$t = 1, \dots, T$. Team $i$ has sites $s$ at buses $k(s)$ with capacities
$C_s, G_s, P_s, B_s$ and service fraction $\alpha_i$ ($\alpha_i = 1$ unless
the fair projection is needed).

\paragraph{Decision variables, for every operating period $t$.} Existing generation
$p_{g,t}$; voltage angles $\theta_{n,t}$ and flows $f_{\ell,t}$; private
generation in three cost segments $q^{m}_{s,t}$, $m = 1,2,3$, with costs
$c^1, c^2, c^3 = 80, 150, 250$ \$/MWh and widths $w^1, w^2, w^3 = 0.25, 0.5,
0.25$; battery net output $u_{s,t}$ in MW (positive means discharging) and
state of charge $e_{s,d,h}$ in MWh. Each day $d$ contains four six-hour
operating periods $h=1,\ldots,4$, and operating period $t=4(d-1)+h$ is the
$h$-th of day $d$; $e_{s,d,h}$ is the stored energy at the end of that
operating period and $e_{s,d,0}$ is the stored energy at the start of day $d$.
All energy terms use the period length $\Delta t = 6$ hours.

\paragraph{Problem.} The evaluator minimizes the total generation cost over the
half-year,
\[
  \min \sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big( \sum_g c_g\, p_{g,t} + \sum_s \sum_{m=1}^{3} c^m q^m_{s,t} \Big)
\]
(multiplying this objective by $\Delta t$ would not change the dispatch),
subject to, for every operating period $t=4(d-1)+h$:
\begin{align*}
  & \sum_{g \text{ at } n} p_{g,t} + \sum_{s:\,k(s)=n} \Big(\sum_m q^m_{s,t} + u_{s,t} - \alpha_{i(s)} C_s\Big)
    + s_{n,t} - d_{n,t}
    = \sum_{\ell:\,o(\ell)=n} f_{\ell,t} - \sum_{\ell:\,r(\ell)=n} f_{\ell,t} && \forall n,\\
  & f_{\ell,t} = b_\ell(\theta_{o(\ell),t} - \theta_{r(\ell),t}),\quad |f_{\ell,t}| \le F_\ell,\quad \theta_{\mathrm{ref},t} = 0 && \forall \ell,\\
  & p_g^{\min} \le p_{g,t} \le p_g^{\max},\qquad 0 \le q^m_{s,t} \le w^m G_s && \forall g, s, m,\\
  & e_{s,d,h} = e_{s,d,h-1} - \Delta t\, u_{s,t},\quad 0 \le e_{s,d,h} \le B_s,\quad |u_{s,t}| \le P_s,\quad \Delta t\, u_{s,t} \le e_{s,d,h-1} && \forall s,\ h=1,\ldots,4,\\
  & e_{s,d,0} = 0.5 B_s, \qquad e_{s,d,4} = 0.5 B_s && \forall s,d,\\
  & \sum_{s \in \text{team } i,\ k(s)=n} u_{s,t} \le \sum_{s \in \text{team } i,\ k(s)=n} \alpha_i C_s && \forall i, n,
\end{align*}
where the last constraint keeps a
team's batteries from discharging more than that team's load at a bus.
Storage couples the four six-hour periods within each day, but not different days. The
evaluator therefore solves one independent linear program per daily block;
each block starts and ends at half charge. The grid operator (instructor) solves it exactly. The
preview drops storage, which makes every operating period independent (\cref{sec:preview}).

\paragraph{Prices and score.} The LMP $\lambda_{n,t}$ is the dual variable of
the balance constraint at bus $n$ (i.e., the first constraint above), clipped at zero. Team $i$'s grid purchase at
bus $n$ is the part of its load that its own generation and batteries do not
cover, $\pi_{i,n,t} = \max\{0,\ \sum_{s} (\alpha_i C_s - \sum_m q^m_{s,t} - u_{s,t})\}$,
summed over its sites at $n$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \text{IT value}_i &= 6{,}500\,\Delta t \sum_t \sum_{s \in i} \alpha_i C_s, &
  \text{energy cost}_i &= \Delta t \sum_t \sum_n \lambda_{n,t}\, \pi_{i,n,t},\\
  \text{gen.\ operating}_i &= \Delta t \sum_t \sum_{s \in i} \sum_m c^m q^m_{s,t},\\
  \text{capital}_i &= \sum_{s \in i,\ \text{new}} \big(25\text{M}\,C_s + 1.5\text{M}\,G_s + 0.4\text{M}\,P_s + 0.25\text{M}\,B_s\big),
\end{align*}
where $\Delta t = 6$ hours, the energy cost applies to Submission 2 only, and
``new'' means built in this submission.

\section{The fair projection in detail}\label{app:projection}

When the combined plan of all valid teams is infeasible, the evaluator reduces
service in four steps. Malformed files have already been set aside, so they
cannot block anyone else.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Caps.} Each site's request is limited to 100\MW{} and each
    team's to 500\MW{}. Then the largest team request is limited to the
    smallest positive team request. Call the capped request of team $i$
    $\hat d_i$.
  \item \textbf{Most total service.} Find service fractions
    $\alpha_i \in [10^{-6}, 1]$ that make the joint dispatch feasible in every
    operating period and maximize the total served load $\sum_i \alpha_i \hat d_i$. Call the
    maximum $L^* := \max_{d_i, \forall i} \sum_i \alpha_i \hat d_i$.
  \item \textbf{Proportional fairness.} Among the fractions whose total is
    within $\max(10^{-6}, 10^{-6}L^*)$ of $L^*$, choose the ones that maximize
    $\sum_i \log(\alpha_i \hat d_i)$.
  \item \textbf{Fallback.} If no joint solution is found, the evaluator tries
    leaving one team out at a time, then each team alone, and records what
    happened.
\end{enumerate}

The logarithm in step 3 is Kelly's proportional fairness~\cite{kelly1998rate}.
At such an allocation, any change that raises one team's service by some
percentage must lower the others' by at least as much in total percentage
terms. The caps exist because without them a team could win a bigger share
simply by asking for more. Step 2 comes first because the grid's capacity is
valuable: the evaluator will not waste headroom just to make shares equal.
That is why, in the two-team example of \cref{sec:twoteam}, the team whose MW
uses less of the bottleneck is served in full.

\section{A little game theory}\label{app:game}

A \emph{game} has players (the teams), strategies (each team's plan), and
payoffs (each team's score), where every payoff depends on everyone's
strategies. Write $u_i(x_i, x_{-i})$ for team $i$'s payoff when it plays strategy $x_i$
and the others play strategies $x_{-i}$, which is defined to be the collection of strategies of teams other than team $i$. Below are some concepts in game theory:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A \textbf{best response} to $x_{-i}$ is a plan $x_i$ that maximizes
    $u_i(x_i, x_{-i})$.
  \item A \textbf{dominant strategy} is a best response to everything the
    others might do. In \cref{sec:twoteam}, ``small'' is dominant for team B.
  \item A \textbf{Nash equilibrium}~\cite{nash1950equilibrium} is a profile
    $x^*$ in which every plan is a best response to the others:
    $u_i(x_i^*, x_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(x_i, x_{-i}^*)$ for every team $i$ and every
    alternative $x_i$. No team can gain by changing its plan alone.
\end{itemize}

Two classic ideas fit this game well. In a \emph{congestion game}, players
share resources whose value falls as more players use them, like roads or, here,
the headroom on transmission lines; such games always have a pure-strategy Nash
equilibrium~\cite{rosenthal1973class}. The \emph{tragedy of the
commons}~\cite{hardin1968tragedy} describes what can happen at that
equilibrium: each player overuses a shared resource because it bears only part
of the cost, and everyone ends up worse off.

The game also unfolds over time. You choose Submission 2 after seeing the
results of Submission 1, and Week 3 after Week 2, so you can learn how the other
teams play and adjust, and so can they. Ben Polak's Game Theory course at Yale
is a free, approachable introduction to all of these
ideas~\cite{polak2007gametheory}.
